## I. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Reasons to choose the research topic

Forecasting asset volatility plays a crucial role across the financial services sector, particular in areas such as risk management, capital allocation, and financial engineering. Empirical evidence indicates that the volatility of financial assets displays highly intricate behavior, including clustering (Mandelbrot, 1967), asymmetric responses to shocks (Black, 1976) and leptokurtosis (Carnero et al., 2004). Such characteristics reflect long-range autocorrelations and nonlinear sequential dependencies, rendering the modeling and prediction of volatility a complex task.

To address this challenge, Engle (1982) introduced the groundbreaking Autoregressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity (ARCH) model. Its core premise is that variance, or volatility, is not constant but depends on the magnitude of past shocks or unexpected news. In other words, negative news from the previous day can increase market volatility in the current period. However, the ARCH model requires a large number of parameters to fully describe long-term volatility cycles. Consequently, Bollerslev (1986) extended Engle's idea to create the Generalized ARCH (GARCH) model. A key breakthrough of GARCH is its ability to effectively capture the "volatility clustering" phenomenon.

Empirical studies (Black, 1976; Carnero et al., 2004). have confirmed the presence of asymmetric market reactions to information, where the impact of bad news on volatility is often significantly stronger than that of good news of equivalent magnitude. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as the "leverage effect," has been integrated into extended GARCH models, such as GJR-GARCH (Glosten et al., 1993) and EGARCH (Nelson, 1991). These models were developed to better capture the asymmetric effects and compositional structure of volatility (Jouilil & Mentagui, 2023).

Beyond endogenous factors, a growing body of research (Alam & Uddin, 2009; M. H. Khan, 2014; Le et al., 2019; Singhal et al., 2019; Smyth & Narayan, 2018) has shown that external factors also affect financial asset volatility. In response, models such as GARCH-X and GARCH-MIDAS (Mixed Data Sampling) have been developed. Among these, the GARCH-X framework (Han & Kristensen, 2014) enables the inclusion of exogenous variables that share the same frequency as asset returns into the conditional variance equation. Meanwhile, the GARCH-MIDAS (Engle et al., 2013) extends this capability by integrating exogenous variables with different frequencies into the GARCH model.

In recent years, combining GARCH models with deep learning techniques has shown significant potential in enhancing the accuracy of volatility forecasts (Song et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). A notable approach is the RECH model proposed by (Nguyen et al., 2022), which is designed to overcome the limitations of traditional linear

GARCH models. Instead of considering only the linear effects of shocks and volatility in return series, RECH employs a recurrent neural network (RNN) to capture the nonlinear effects of shocks and past return information. Empirical results indicate that RECH achieves superior forecasting performance compared to the GARCH model.

Building upon these foundations, the study proposes an enhanced RECH model that incorporates exogenous factors into the deep learning network to further improve asset volatility forecasting performance. The proposed model is expected not only to enhance forecasting accuracy but also to help explain the influence of exogenous factors on asset price volatility.

Moreover, this study extends the GARCH-MIDAS model (Engle et al., 2013) by incorporating a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997). The proposed approach draws on the rationale of the RECH model (Nguyen et al., 2022), leveraging the GARCH-MIDAS framework to decompose volatility components while harnessing the predictive power of LSTM. Empirically, this study applies the proposed models to assess the impact of domestic macroeconomic indicators and global financial indicators on volatility forecasts in the Vietnamese stock market.

### 1.2 Research objectives and research questions

#### 1.2.1 General objectives

The primary objective of this thesis is to develop hybrid models that integrates deep learning architectures, specifically Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) with established econometric volatility models such as GARCH, GARCH-MIDAS, ..., and regularized regressions like Lasso. This synergistic integration aims to develop robust hybrid models, capitalizing on the complementary strengths of both frameworks to analyze the impacts of macroeconomic factors and financial indicators on asset return volatility.

By fusing the non-linear pattern recognition capabilities of deep learning with the theoretical rigor of econometrics, the proposed models are designed to more effectively capture complex, non-linear dependencies and structural breaks in financial data. This leads to enhanced volatility forecasting accuracy, directly addressing a key limitation of traditional econometric models, which often rely on pre-specified linear structures and struggle with highly complex data patterns.

This research is driven by two primary objectives. First, it seeks to augment an existing hybrid framework, the Recurrent Conditional Heteroskedasticity (RECH) model (Nguyen et al., 2022), by formally integrating exogenous covariates into its architecture. This systematic incorporation is anticipated to enhance the model's capacity to capture the influence of nuanced fundamental and macroeconomic drivers on time-varying volatility. Consequently, the augmentation is expected to yield not only improved forecasting accuracy but also forecasts with greater economic interpretability.

Second, this study is conducted to refine the Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity-Mixed Data Sampling (GARCH-MIDAS) econometric model (Asgharian et al., 2013). To overcome the model's limitations in capturing complex non-linear relationships, we propose a novel method for integrating deep learning architectures. Specifically, our approach directly integrates a deep neural network into the GARCH-MIDAS framework, which differs from studies that merely use GARCH model estimates as feature extraction tools. In those studies, the extracted features serve as inputs for deep learning models to forecast volatility. Thanks to this direct integration, our model can explain the influence of the covariates, thereby addressing the "black box" problem commonly associated with feature-based methodologies.

To underpin these structural enhancements, a core focus of this research is the development of robust parameter estimation techniques tailored for these hybrid models. We will leverage a comprehensive suite of advanced methods, including Bayesian and Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) for statistical inference, alongside optimization algorithms such as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam). Supporting tools like Lasso regularization and gradient clipping will be employed to ensure model stability and prevent overfitting. This rigorous methodological foundation is designed to ensure precise model calibration and generate reliable, robust predictions.

### 3.1 Literature reviews

#### 3.1.1. Traditional volatility forecasting models

Understanding and accurately quantifying volatility is fundamental to option pricing, risk management, portfolio construction, and testing the efficient market hypothesis. This enables investors and financial institutions to make better-informed decisions in uncertain environments. Volatility, which measures the magnitude of asset price fluctuations, exhibits key characteristics that cannot be fully captured by simple statistical models. First, volatility is nonlinear, implying that market reactions to shocks are often disproportionate, thereby limiting the applicability of traditional linear models. Second, and most notably, volatility tends to cluster periods of high volatility are likely to be followed by further high volatility, and similarly for low volatility (Mandelbrot, 1967). This suggests that current volatility possesses predictive power over future volatility. Third, asset return distributions frequently display heavy tails (Carnero et al., 2004), meaning that the likelihood of extreme events (such as substantial gains or losses) is significantly greater than what would be anticipated under a normal distribution. It is the coexistence of these complexities that underscores the necessity for advanced volatility modeling.

The introduction of ARCH/GARCH models and their subsequent variants has effectively addressed these phenomena. The development of Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models marked a pivotal advancement in econometrics, building upon Robert Engle's seminal (Engle, 1982) work on the Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (ARCH) model. Engle's ARCH model fundamentally transformed financial time series analysis by providing a formal framework to capture a crucial empirical feature known as volatility clustering the tendency for large price movements to be followed by further large movements, while periods of calm persist. Acknowledging the practical limitations of ARCH models, particularly their frequent requirement for numerous parameters to describe volatility processes, (Bollerslev, 1986) introduced the generalized GARCH model. This model elegantly incorporates both lagged conditional variances and lagged squared errors, enabling the capture of persistent volatility dynamics with greater parsimony. This breakthrough led to the foundational GARCH(p, q) specification, which rapidly became an essential tool for modeling and forecasting financial market volatility.

However, a key limitation of standard ARCH/GARCH models lies in their exclusive focus on conditional variance while neglecting the influence of return direction on volatility. This assumption contradicts empirical evidence, which frequently reveals asymmetric effects: negative return shocks in financial markets tend to increase volatility more than positive shocks of the same magnitude (Black, 1976; Christie, 1982). This "leverage effect" is particularly pronounced in equity indices. Consequently,

asymmetric GARCH models such as EGARCH (Nelson, 1991), GJR-GARCH (Glosten et al., 1993) and TGARCH (Zakoian, 1994) were developed to more accurately quantify this complex relationship. Furthermore, Engle et al. (1987) proposed the GARCH-in-Mean (GARCH-M) model, wherein the conditional variance (representing risk) serves as an explanatory variable in the mean equation. This formulation allows for a direct assessment of the risk-return trade-off, aligning more closely with financial theory.

Unlike GARCH models, stochastic volatility (SV) models (Taylor, 1994) are more flexible in fitting financial time series data (Yu et al., 2014). However, SV models have limitations in terms of computation, estimation, and inference. This makes SV models less popular than the GARCH family of models in many practical applications. Compared to SV models, GARCH models are observation-based, which have the advantage of being intuitive, simple, and easy to implement. Therefore, volatility modeling and forecasting mainly rely on GARCH models (Granger & Poon, 2001; P. Hansen & Lunde, 2001).

The application of GARCH models remains widespread, as has been emphasized in various studies. Medeiros & Matsumoto, (2005) conducted a study focusing on the Brazilian stock market, examining stock returns associated with equity issuance announcements. Their findings highlight the importance of understanding market reactions and volatility, suggesting that GARCH models can play an important role in analyzing such events due to their ability to model volatility over time. Al Najjar (2016) examined stock return volatility on the Amman Stock Exchange (ASE) over the period 2005-2014; Lin et al. (2017) studied the SSE Composite Index in the Chinese stock market. The results of both studies confirm the existence of typical econometric characteristics such as clustering volatility and leptokurtic distribution of market return series. By comparing the forecasting performance, the authors found that the asymmetric EGARCH(1,1) model fits and forecasts better than the GARCH(1,1) and TARCH(1,1) models for the Chinese stock market. Jange (2023) uses the GARCH model to forecast the volatility of the Indonesian Composite Index (JKSE) based on historical data from 2012 to 2022. The study shows a prediction accuracy with a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 17.26%. Notably, volatility increases significantly in May 2022 due to the Eid holiday, while it decreases in July and continues to decrease in September due to government policy changes. GARCH and SV volatility models are considered standard models because of their ability to capture some volatility dynamics over time. However, these models still do not capture external sources of information about asset volatility. Exogenous sources of information can be realized volatility or economic news that signals changes in financial market conditions. For example, realized volatility (RV) estimated using high-frequency data is more informative about current volatility levels than daily squared returns (Andersen et al., 2001; Hansen et al., 2012, 2014; Koopman et al., 2005). In addition, incorporating economic news instead of just using return time

series in traditional econometric volatility models can increase predictive power (Conrad & Kleen, 2020; Engle et al., 2013).

#### 3.1.2. Incorporating exogenous information into volatility models

Models in the GARCH and SV classes typically assume that input and output data share the same frequency, such as a daily frequency. However, in practice, many macroeconomic and financial variables relevant to market volatility are published at lower frequencies: monthly, quarterly, or annually. This creates a research gap: since volatility analyses often rely on high-frequency data, they have primarily incorporated only variables available at a daily frequency (such as short-term interest rates, term premiums, and default premiums). Consequently, the impact of key indicators like unemployment or inflation which are typically observed at monthly or quarterly intervals has not been fully assessed. Previous studies have identified links between macroeconomic variables and market volatility. For example, Schwert (1989) found that variables such as bond returns, short-term interest rates, and industrial production growth rates could explain monthly market volatility. Other works, such as those by Brandt et al., 2002; Glosten et al., 1993; Hamilton & Lin, 1996; Perez-Quiros & Timmermann, 2000; Whitelaw, 1994 have also affirmed the significant role of factors such as interest rates, the state of the economy, and various risk premiums in explaining market variance. To address the issue of mixed-frequency data, Ghysels et al. (2006) introduced the MIDAS (Mixed Data Sampling) regression method, which allows data of different frequencies to be incorporated within a single model. Building on this framework, Engle et al. (2009) developed the GARCH-MIDAS model, in which conditional variance is decomposed into two components: a long-term component (influenced by low-frequency variables) and a short-term component. Macroeconomic variables (e.g., monthly data) are transformed to a higher frequency (e.g., daily) via beta polynomial or exponential weighting functions, enabling their integration into traditional GARCH-type volatility modeling. This approach combines the component model idea of Engle & Lee (1999) with the MIDAS framework of Ghysels et al. (2006). It allows the separation of volatility dynamics into long-term and short-term factors and captures the long-memory property of volatility, as evidenced by Maheu (2007) and Ding & Granger, (1996). Thanks to MIDAS, exogenous explanatory variables can now be directly linked to market volatility. Several studies, including Wang & Ghysels (2015) and Conrad & Kleen (2020), have demonstrated the model's ability to capture complex volatility dynamics such as persistence, structural breaks, and clustered variations.

The GARCH-MIDAS framework has gained prominence in empirical finance for its ability to integrate low-frequency macroeconomic information into high-frequency volatility modeling. The seminal work by Engle et al. (2013) established this paradigm, demonstrating that the long-run component of U.S. stock market volatility can be effectively captured by macroeconomic indicators such as industrial production and

unemployment. Building on this foundation, Conrad & Loch (2015) conducted a comprehensive variable selection exercise, identifying term spreads and housing starts as the most predictive variables for long-run stock market volatility, while survey-based expectations for industrial production and unemployment also provided significant explanatory power. In a similar vein, Asgharian et al. (2013) expanded the set of potential predictors to include a wide array of financial and macroeconomic variables (e.g., interest rates, inflation, exchange rates). Their key contribution was to empirically validate that incorporating such low-frequency data within the GARCH-MIDAS structure consistently yields superior volatility forecasts compared to traditional GARCH models. Beyond these empirical applications, Wang & Ghysels (2015) strengthened the framework's methodological rigor by developing formal statistical tests specifically for volatility component models, thereby providing essential tools for reliable inference in subsequent studies.

#### 3.1.3. Deep learning in volatility forecasting

The growing complexity of financial markets, exemplified by the rise of highly volatile assets like cryptocurrencies, has highlighted the limitations of traditional linear parametric models in capturing intricate nonlinear relationships and long-term dependencies within financial time series Fischer & Krauss (2018). Concurrently, the advent of Deep learning has introduced powerful architectures such as Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs). These models excel at automatically learning complex patterns from sequential data without relying on rigid functional-form assumptions. Numerous studies (Cai et al., 2023; Hamid & Iqbal, 2004; Pallotta & Ciciretti, 2023) have demonstrated the superior forecast accuracy of pure Deep learning models for volatility across diverse asset classes, from traditional GARCH model.

However, pure Deep learning models are frequently criticized as "black boxes," lacking the econometric foundation and theoretical grounding inherent to models like GARCH. This can lead to a potential oversight of well-established financial stylized facts Fischer & Krauss (2018). To bridge this gap, hybrid models have emerged as a strategic and promising research paradigm. The core objective of these models is to synergistically combine the strengths of both approaches: the ability of GARCH-family models to capture key statistical properties (e.g., volatility clustering, leptokurtosis) with the representational power of Deep learning to model complex nonlinear structures (Kim & Won, 2018; Kristjanpoller et al., 2014; Kristjanpoller & Hernández, 2017; Lu et al., 2016).

The advent of Deep Learning, particularly through architectures such as RNN, LSTM and GRU has provided powerful tools for learning complex patterns from time-series data. However, purely DL-based models can sometimes lack econometric rigor and may fail to leverage the well-established financial intuition offered by traditional models (Fischer & Krauss, 2018).

#### 3.1.4 Hybrid models: Bridging Econometrics and Deep learning

Hybrid models have emerged to bridge this gap, aiming to combine the best of both worlds: the ability of GARCH-type models to capture the inherent statistical characteristics of volatility, and the representational strength of DL to learn complex nonlinear patterns. To operationalize the synergy between econometric rigor and representational power, recent research has developed various hybrid architectures. These can be categorized into three principal paradigms based on their integration mechanism:

Sequential Hybrid Models: This architecture employs a two-stage process where outputs from a GARCH-type model (e.g., conditional variance, standardized residuals) serve as input features for a subsequent deep learning model, such as an LSTM or ANN. The underlying assumption is that the Deep learning component can learn and correct for residual nonlinear patterns not captured by the initial linear GARCH framework. Representative applications include ANN-GARCH and LSTM-GARCH models for volatility forecasting in equities and commodities (Hu et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2016).

Ensemble Hybrid Models: This approach treats forecasts from standalone GARCH and Deep learning models as independent predictions. These are subsequently combined using an aggregation mechanism, such as weighted averaging or a meta-learner, to leverage the distinct strengths of each methodology and produce a more robust and accurate consensus forecast (Kristjanpoller et al., 2014).

Deeply Integrated Frameworks: The most architecturally sophisticated paradigm involves directly embedding the core principles of GARCH into the neural network's structure. Frameworks such as "Heteroscedastic Neural Networks" or "GARCH-based Mixture Recurrent Networks" exemplify this trend, creating a unified model capable of jointly learning canonical statistical properties and complex nonlinear dependencies.

Empirical evidence across diverse asset classes including equities (Kim & Won, 2018; Liu et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2022), commodities (Hu et al., 2020), and cryptocurrencies (Amirshahi & Lahmiri, 2023; Michańków et al., 2024) consistently indicates that hybrid GARCH-DL models generally achieve significantly superior forecasting accuracy compared to their individual constituents, as measured by error metrics like RMSE and MAE. Furthermore, these hybrids demonstrate enhanced capability in processing complex data structures, such as mixed-frequency (Ma et al., 2024) and intraday data, and can effectively incorporate unstructured data sources like news sentiment to further boost predictive power.

Nevertheless, the application of these models requires careful consideration. Their performance is not universal and can be contingent upon specific asset characteristics, prevailing market regimes, and the forecast horizon. Additionally, their adoption entails

significantly higher computational complexity and data requirements compared to traditional GARCH models.

#### 3.1.5 Synergistic advantages of GARCH-Deep learning hybrid models

Combining GARCH models with deep learning architectures creates a powerful hybrid framework that synergizes the strengths of both paradigms for superior volatility forecasting. This integration addresses key limitations inherent in each approach when used in isolation. The primary advantages of GARCH-Deep learning (GARCH-DL) hybrids are threefold.

First, they achieve enhanced forecasting accuracy by leveraging complementary strengths. Traditional GARCH models provide a robust, theory driven foundation for capturing well documented stylized facts like volatility clustering and persistence. However, they often rely on linear or pre-specified nonlinear structures. Deep learning components, such as RNN and LSTM, excel at identifying complex, non-linear patterns and long-term dependencies directly from data. By using the conditional variance estimated by GARCH as a refined input feature, the Deep learning component can model residual nonlinearities and structural breaks more effectively than either model alone, leading to lower forecast errors (e.g., MAE, MSE) in various markets (Michałków et al., 2024).

Second, this hybrid approach improves model adaptability and robustness. Pure GARCH models can be rigid and slow to adapt to new market regimes, potentially overpredicting volatility during calm periods or under-predicting during crises. Deep learning models, by design, are highly adaptive. In a hybrid setup, the Deep learning layer continuously learns from evolving data patterns, allowing the combined model to adjust its forecasts dynamically. This balances the tendencies of each component, resulting in more reliable and stable predictions across different market conditions.

Third, the framework uniquely enables the integration of multimodal and unstructured data. Traditional GARCH models are limited to structured, numerical time-series. The Deep learning arm of a hybrid model, however, can process diverse inputs such as news sentiment, social media metrics, or macroeconomic text reports through embedding layers or convolutional neural network (CNN). These processed features can be fused with GARCH-based volatility estimates to capture the impact of market sentiment and breaking news on volatility dynamics a dimension purely econometric models cannot access. This makes GARCH-DL hybrids particularly suited for modern markets where news and sentiment drive short-term volatility spikes (Hicham & Karim, 2023).

Fourth, the framework facilitates more sophisticated risk assessment. While improved point forecasts for volatility are a key outcome, advanced hybrids have also demonstrated potential in refining tail-risk measures. By more accurately modeling the

entire distribution of forecasted volatility, these models can generate more precise estimates for critical risk metrics like Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES), although this remains an area for ongoing refinement to consistently outperform traditional methods in all risk dimensions.

A Note on Interpretability and Model Complexity. It is important to acknowledge that the enhanced power of GARCH-DL hybrids comes with a trade-off in interpretability. While the GARCH component preserves a layer of econometric transparency, the overall model remains more complex and less interpretable than a standalone GARCH model. However, this hybrid architecture offers a structured pathway toward partial interpretability. Analysts can examine the GARCH-derived inputs (e.g., conditional variance) to understand the theory-grounded foundation, while attributing further predictive refinements to the Deep learning layer's ability to capture intangible patterns. Emerging techniques like attention mechanisms (Bao, Yue, & Rao, 2017; Miami-Namini, Tavakoli, & Namin, 2019) can be integrated to shed light on which historical periods the model prioritizes, thereby mitigating, though not eliminating, the "black-box" concern.

In conclusion, the GARCH-DL hybrid paradigm does not merely stack two models but creates a cohesive system where econometric theory guides feature engineering and deep learning provides adaptive, high-dimensional function approximation. This synergy makes it a particularly potent tool for navigating the complex, non-linear dynamics of modern financial markets.

#### 3.1.6. Research graph

A comprehensive review of the literature confirms that volatility forecasting in finance is a dynamic research field characterized by diverse methodological approaches. These approaches share the common objectives of capturing the properties of return series, enhancing forecasting accuracy, and improving the explanatory power for fluctuations in return sequences. Despite significant advancements, two critical research gaps persist and form the foundational basis for this study.

The first gap concerns the integration of macroeconomic and financial information into deep learning-based hybrid models. The hybrid approach, which combines deep neural networks with traditional GARCH models to capture long-term and nonlinear volatility components, represents a highly promising research direction. A prominent example is the work of Nguyen et al. (2022), who proposed the RECH model utilizing a RNN to learn nonlinear features and long-term dependencies from historical return and variance data. Empirical findings demonstrate that RECH outperforms traditional econometric models (Nguyen et al., 2022). However, a fundamental limitation of the RECH model and similar deep learning architectures is their primary reliance on pure time-series data, thereby overlooking the influence of macroeconomic indicators (e.g., interest rates, inflation) and financial indices (e.g., the VIX fear index). A substantial

body of empirical research has consistently confirmed the significant impact of these exogenous factors on market volatility (e.g., Conrad & Loch, 2015; Engle et al., 2013). Consequently, a critical research gap exists: the necessity to develop a hybrid model that not only captures the nonlinear characteristics and long-term "memory" of the data but also effectively integrates diverse exogenous covariates to simultaneously enhance both explanatory power and forecasting accuracy.

The second gap stems from the inherent limitations of the GARCH-MIDAS model. This model marks a significant advancement by enabling the combination of low-frequency data (e.g., monthly macroeconomic variables) with high-frequency data (e.g., daily returns) for volatility modeling (Engle et al., 2013). Nonetheless, GARCH-MIDAS inherits a core weakness from traditional GARCH frameworks: its limited capacity to capture nonlinear relationships. Furthermore, as the number of exogenous covariates and their lag orders increases, the model encounters the curse of dimensionality. This leads to a rapid explosion in the number of parameters requiring estimation, complicating the estimation process and exacerbating the risk of overfitting. To address these dual challenges concurrently, this study proposes a two-pronged approach: (1) Implementing the LASSO method (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) to perform automated variable selection and model regularization, thereby mitigating high-dimensionality issues and improving model stability and (2) Integrating an LSTM network such as a superior deep learning architecture for time-series data, proven highly effective in modeling complex, long-term nonlinear dependencies (Fischer & Krauss, 2018; Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) to augment the modeling of the short-term volatility component. This synergistic combination promises to yield a novel LSTM-GARCH-MIDAS-LASSO hybrid model that is both robust in its modeling capabilities and computationally efficient.

In summary, this research aims to bridge these two gaps by proposing and evaluating: (1) the enrichment of a deep learning hybrid framework through the incorporation of economic-financial variables as exogenous covariates; and (2) a novel hybrid architecture that leverages the combined strengths of LSTM, GARCH-MIDAS, and LASSO to achieve superior forecasting performance compared to established benchmark models.

### 3.2. Theoritical framework

The research of this thesis builds on three main areas: Conditional heteroscedastic models, Deep learning and Recurrent Conditional Heteroskedastic.

#### 3.2.1 Conditional heteroscedastic models

A significant concern when it comes to forecasting socio-economic issues, especially for investors, is risk prediction. Risk can be defined in various ways and measured using multiple indicators. In the field of econometrics, risk refers to the

conditional variance of the return (also known as the expected rate of return) of a specific asset. Autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity models (ARCH) and the generalized ARCH model (GARCH) have emerged as crucial tools in estimating and forecasting risk. They are valued for their capability to simulate pronounced fluctuations and account for variance heterogeneity.

The estimation and forecasting of risk are common challenges within the domain of finance and investment. Models such as ARCH and GARCH offer a means to measure and manage the risk associated with stocks, investment funds, and other financial instruments. By predicting variance, these models assist investors and risk managers in identifying and adjusting suitable investment allocations and risk management strategies.

Rate of Return in one period  $ [t-1, t] $ of an asset is denoted and determined by the formula:  $ y_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} $, where  $ P_t $ is the price of the asset at the end of period t. In reality, when the yield calculation cycle is quite short (session, trading day, week), the yield is quite small, so people often approximate asset yield according to the following formula:  $ y_t \approx \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) $.

The volatility of an asset can be measured by the variance of its return, denoted as  $ \sigma^{2} $, or its standard deviation, represented by  $ \sigma $, over a specific period. Higher volatility indicates greater price fluctuations, making the asset riskier to hold. Therefore, the volatility of an asset can be utilized as a reflection of its level of risk.

##### 3.2.1.1 GARCH

The GARCH model (generalized ARCH model) was first proposed by Bollerslev (1986) to overcome the limitation of many lags of the ARCH model. It is because, a model with high lag will likely affect the estimation results by significantly reducing the number of degrees of freedom in the model. This is even more important for short time series. Therefore, the GARCH model tends to be more commonly used by forecasters. GARCH models are increasingly becoming important tools in time series data analysis, especially applied in the financial sector.

The GARCH(p, q) model has the following form:

 $$ y_{t}=\sigma_{t}.\varepsilon_{t},t=1,2,\ldots,T, $$ 

 $$ \sigma_{t}^{2}=w+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}y_{t-i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\sigma_{t-j}^{2},t=2,\ldots,T. $$ 

Where:  $ \varepsilon_t $ are independent and identical distributed – iid;  $ w > 0 $,  $ \alpha_j \geq 0 $,  $ \beta_i \geq 0 $ and  $ \sum_{i=1}^{\max(p,q)}(\alpha_i + \beta_i) < 1 $,  $ (i = 1, \ldots, p; j = 1, \ldots, q) $ (Tsay, 2005) to ensure the stationarity of the GARCH process. If  $ p > q $ then  $ \alpha_i = 0 $ with  $ i > q $. If  $ q > p $ then  $ \beta_i = 0 $ with  $ i > p $.

##### 3.2.1.2 GARCH-X

A straight forward extension of the GARCH model is a GARCH-X which incorporates related exogenous information for volatility modeling:

 $$ y_{t}=\sigma_{t}.\varepsilon_{t},t=1,2,\ldots,T, $$ 

 $$ \sigma_{t}^{2}=w+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}y_{t-i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\sigma_{t-j}^{2}+\pi^{T}x_{t-1},t=2,\ldots,T. $$ 

Here,  $ x_t $ is the vector of non-negative exogenous variables and  $ \pi $ the vector of non-negative coefficients. For negative  $ x_t $, one often replaces  $ \pi^T x_{t-1} $, with  $ \pi^T x_{t-1}^2 $ (Francq & Thieu, 2019). In this model, suppose that  $ x_t > 0 $ for all  $ t, \omega, \alpha_i, \beta_j, \pi $ are non-negative model parameters. Among these parameters,  $ \pi $ specifically represents a vector that establishes the relationship between the scalar product and the input vector  $ x_t $. This  $ x_t $ vector comprises additional information sources.

##### 3.2.1.3 Real GARCH

When high-frequency data are available, one of the most successful econometric models that incorporate RV into volatility modeling is perhaps the non-exponential RealGARCH model of Hansen et al. (2012):

 $$ y_{t}=\sigma_{t}\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{t}\sim T(\nu),\varepsilon_{t}i i d,t=1,2,...,T, $$ 

 $$ \sigma_{t}^{2}=\omega+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^{2}+\gamma R V_{t-1},t=2,\ldots,T. $$ 

 $$ RV_{t}=\xi+\varphi\sigma_{t}^{2}+\tau_{1}\varepsilon_{t}+\tau_{2}\left(\frac{\nu-2}{\nu}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}-1\right)+d_{t}, $$ 

 $$ t=1,\ldots,T,u_{t}{\sim}i i d,u_{t}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_{u}^{2}). $$ 

The model parameters include  $ \nu $,  $ \omega $,  $ \beta $,  $ \gamma $,  $ \xi $,  $ \varphi $,  $ \tau_1 $,  $ \tau_2 $ and  $ \sigma_u^2 $. To ensure that  $ \sigma_t^2 $ is finite and positive, the following conditions are proposed:  $ \omega + \gamma\xi > 0 $ and  $ 0 < \beta + \gamma\varphi < 1 $; see Gerlach & Wang, 2016; Hansen et al., 2012 for more details.

##### 3.2.1.4 GARCH-MIDAS

The GARCH-MIDAS model is proposed as a means to analyze the volatility of market dynamics that vary over time (Engle et al., 2013). The model is developed within the MIDAS framework, which stands for "Mixed Data Sampling". This framework divides the conditional variance into two components: long-term and short-term.

One notable advantage of the GARCH-MIDAS model is its ability to establish a direct link between daily observations on stock returns and macroeconomic variables that are sampled at lower frequencies. By doing so, it enables researchers to examine and quantify the impact of macroeconomic variables on stock volatility. This integration of different frequency data provides valuable insights into the relationship between macroeconomic factors and stock market dynamics. GARCH-MIDAS model can formally be described as below. Assume the return on day i in month t follows the following process:

 $$ y_{i,t}=\sqrt{\tau_{t}g_{i,t}}\varepsilon_{i,t},i=1,\ldots,D_{t};\varepsilon_{i,t}|F_{i-1,t}\sim N(0,1), $$ 

where $D_{t}$ is the number of trading days in month $t$ and $F_{i-1,t}$ is the information set up to $(i-1)$th day of period $t$. The variance is divided into two components: a short-term component represented by $g_{i,t}$ and a long-term component represented by $\tau_{t}$. The conditional variance dynamics of the component follow $g_{i,t}$ (daily) GARCH$(1,1)$ process, as:

 $$ g_{i,t}=(1-\alpha-\beta)+\alpha\frac{y_{i-1,t}^{2}}{\tau_{t}}+\beta g_{i-1,t}. $$ 

The component  $ \tau_{t} $ is defined as long-term volatility component with explanatory variables in the spirit of MIDAS regression:

 $$ \log(\tau_{t})=m+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{k}\left(w_{j,1},w_{j,2}\right)x_{j,t-k}, $$ 

where J is number of explanatory variables and  $ \theta_{j} $ measures the impact of the  $ x_{j,t-k} $ variable on the long-term volatility; K is the number of periods over which we smooth the volatility. Finally, the total conditional variance can be defined as:

 $$ \sigma_{i,t}^{2}=\tau_{t}g_{i,t}. $$ 

The weights  $ \phi_k $ are completely determined by two parameters  $ w_{j,1} $ and  $ w_{j,2} $. It is easy to find that  $ \phi_k \geq 0 $ for  $ k = 1, 2, \ldots, K $ and  $ \sum_{k=1}^K \phi_k = 1 $.

#### 3.2.2 Deep learning model for volatility forecasting

##### 3.2.2.1 Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are specialized class of artificial neural networks designed for processing sequential or time series data. Its most distinctive feature is an internal memory mechanism, implemented through a hidden state that retains and transmits information from previous time steps to the next. This architecture allows the network's output at each step to be a function of both the current input and the aggregated information from all preceding steps, enabling RNN to capture essential temporal dependencies. The fundamental operations of a standard RNN, as formalized by Elman (1990), can be described by the following core equations:

 $$ h_{t}=\Psi(W_{h}[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{h}), $$ 

where  $ x_{t} $ is the input,  $ h_{t} $ the set of hidden states at time t, and  $ \Psi $ is an activation function. The learnable parameters  $ W_{h} $ and  $ b_{h} $ are matrices and vectors of suitable sizes. This standard RNN often fails to learn from long sequences due to vanishing gradient problem (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). To overcome this fundamental issue, more complex variants such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) have been introduced.

##### 3.2.2.2 Long Short-Term Memory

The LSTM architecture, introduced by (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997), is specifically engineered to solve the vanishing gradient problem and to capture longrange dependencies in sequential data. Its core innovation is a gated memory cell, which allows the network to precisely control the flow of information selectively storing, updating, or discarding information over many time steps. The functionality of an LSTM cell at each time step t is governed by several interdependent gates, input gate  $ i_t $, forget gate  $ f_t $ and output gate  $ o_t $. Mathematically, the hidden state  $ h_t $ is updated as follows:

 $$ \begin{aligned}&f_{t}=\sigma\big(W_{f}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{f}\big),\\&i_{t}=\sigma(W_{i}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{i}),\\&\tilde{C}_{t}=\tanh(W_{C}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{C}).\\&C_{t}=f_{t}\odot C_{t-1}+i_{t}\odot\tilde{C}_{t},\\&o_{t}=\sigma(W_{o}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{o}),\\&h_{t}=o_{t}\odot\tanh(C_{t}).\\ \end{aligned} $$ 

Here,  $ \sigma(\cdot) $ and  $ \tanh(\cdot) $ denote the sigmoid and  $ \tanh $ activations. Furthermore,  $ \odot $ denotes the Hadamard product between two vectors of the same dimension.

##### 3.2.2.3 Gated Recurrent Unit

For initialization, it is standard to set  $ h_{1}=0 $, i.e., the neural network initially has no memory. Alternatively, Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) was introduced by Cho et al. (2014) as a streamlined version of LSTM network. This model integrates the cell state and hidden state into a single entity, simplifying the internal architecture. Rather than employing the three gates mechanism of LSTM, GRU utilizes only two primary gates: the update gate and the reset gate. This simplification reduces the number of trainable parameters and accelerates training, while matching or even surpassing performance of LSTM in tasks such as time series forecasting. GRU is defined as

 $$ \begin{aligned}z_{t}&=\sigma(W_{z}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{z}),\\r_{t}&=\sigma(W_{r}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{r}),\\\tilde{h}_{t}&=\tanh(W_{h}\cdot[x_{t},h_{t-1}]+b_{h}),\\h_{t}&=(1-z_{t})\odot h_{t-1}+z_{t}\odot\tilde{h}_{t}.\end{aligned} $$ 

#### 3.2.3 Recurrent Conditional Heteroskedastic model

The RECH models proposed by Nguyen et al. (2022) are a class of volatility models that integrate a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) into a GARCH framework for flexible volatility modeling. Their key motivation is to use an additive component, governed by an RNN, to capture complex serial dependence structures in volatility dynamics that might be overlooked by the standard GARCH component.

The general RECH model is:

 $$ y_{t}=\sigma_{t}\varepsilon_{t},\varepsilon_{t}\sim T(\nu),\varepsilon_{t}is i.i.d,t=1,2,...,T, $$ 

 $$ \sigma_{t}^{2}=g(\omega_{t})+f\big(\sigma_{t-1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{t-p}^{2};y_{t-1,\ldots,}y_{t-q}\big),t=q+1,\ldots,T $$ 

 $$ \omega_{t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}h_{t},\mathbf{t}=1,\ldots,T, $$ 

 $$ h_{t}=\mathrm{RNN}(x_{t},h_{t-1}),t=2,\ldots,T,with h_{1}\equiv0. $$ 

Here,  $ f\left(\sigma_{t-1}^2, \ldots, \sigma_{t-p}^2; y_{t-1}, \ldots, y_{t-q}\right) $ is the GARCH-type component with  $ p $ and  $ q $ the lag orders of  $ \sigma_t^2 $ and  $ y_t $ respectively. The function  $ g(\omega_t) $ is called the recurrent component with  $ \omega_t $ governed by an RNN, where  $ g(\cdot) $ is an activation function;  $ \beta_0 $ and  $ \beta_1 $ are the coefficients. The input vector  $ x_t $ in the calculation of the recurrent state  $ h_t $ is a vector of additional information sources whose choice is discussed shortly. The RECH model reduces to the GARCH model if  $ \beta_1 = 0 $ and  $ g(\omega_t) = \omega_t $.

The recurrent state  $ h_{t} $ in (9d) can be modeled by any RNN framework. Nguyen et al., (2022) use a simple RNN and Liu et al. (2023) opt for the long-short term memory (LSTM) model of Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, (1997) as an alternative. The LSTM uses gating mechanisms to control the flow of information through the network and enable the RNN to selectively retain and forget information over long time horizons. Liu et al. (2023) demonstrate that using the LSTM in general improves the predictive performance of RECH. However, the simple RNN has a simpler structure that allows for the interpretation of the influencing variables and requires less computational cost compared to LSTM.

### 3.3. Methodology

This research adopts a quantitative approach, focusing on the development and implementation of hybrid models for volatility forecasting across multiple stock markets. Due to the structural complexity of the proposed models, particularly the challenges associated with high dimensional parameters and model convergence the estimation process integrates multiple estimation techniques to ensure robust parameter estimates. Following the estimation stage, the out-of-sample forecasting performance of the proposed hybrid models is assessed and compared against traditional benchmark models using a set of established forecast evaluation metrics.

#### 3.3.1 Bayesian inference

Bayesian inference is a fundamental statistical approach for data analysis, which allows us to learn from data in a principled way. In practical data analysis, we often need to estimate unknown quantities based on a sequence of observed data. Within the Bayesian framework, one starts with prior knowledge encapsulated in a prior distribution over the unknown parameters (or latent variables) of interest, and a likelihood function that connects these unknowns to the observed data. By applying Bayes' theorem, we obtain the posterior distribution of the unknown parameters. This posterior distribution forms the basis for all subsequent inferences about the quantities of interest.

In this research, we use sequential Monte Carlo method to estimate parameters in the model. Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC), commonly known as Particle Filtering, is a class of Monte Carlo methods designed for the sequential estimation of the probability distributions of hidden states in a state-space model. SMC is particularly powerful for non-linear and non-Gaussian models, where analytical solutions (e.g., the Kalman filter) are intractable (Doucet et al., 2001, 2013).

Basic State-Space Model:

- State Equation (Transition):  $ x_t \sim f(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) $ models the hidden dynamics.

- Observation Equation:  $ y_t \sim g(y_t \mid x_t) $ models the relationship between the hidden state and the observed data.

The core goal at each time $t$ is to estimate the filtering distribution $p(x_t \mid y_{1:t})$, i.e., the distribution of the current state $x_t$ given all observations up to that time $y_{1:t}$. Instead of computing the distribution analytically, SMC approximates it using a set of weighted particles:

 $$ p(x_{t}\mid y_{1:t})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{t}^{(i)}\delta\big(x_{t}-x_{t}^{(i)}\big), $$ 

where: $\{x_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ is a set of $N$ particles, each representing a hypothesis about the system state; $\{w_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ are the corresponding importance weights, normalized so that $\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^{(i)} = 1$; $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac delta function.

The Basic SMC Algorithm (Particle Filter with Importance Sampling & Resampling)

The standard SMC algorithm operates through a predict-update cycle at each time step t:

- Prediction Step (Particle Propagation): Particles are propagated forward by sampling from a proposal distribution, often chosen as the state transition model:

 $$ x_{t}^{(i)}\sim q\big(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1}^{(i)},y_{t}\big)(Commonly q=f(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1}^{(i)}). $$ 

- Update Step (Weight Update): Each particle's weight is updated based on its consistency with the new observation  $ y_{t} $, utilizing importance sampling:

 $$ \widetilde{w}_{t}^{(i)}=w_{t-1}^{(i)}\cdot\frac{g(y_{t}\mid x_{t}^{(i)})f(x_{t}^{(i)}\mid x_{t-1}^{(i)})}{q(x_{t}^{(i)}\mid x_{t-1}^{(i)},y_{t})}\propto w_{t-1}^{(i)}\cdot g(y_{t}\mid x_{t}^{(i)})(\mathrm{if}q=f) $$ 

where, weights are then normalized:  $ w_{t}^{(i)} = \widetilde{w}_{t}^{(i)} / \sum_{j=1}^{N} \widetilde{w}_{t}^{(j)} $.

- Resampling Step (to combat degeneracy): A key challenge is weight degeneracy, where only a few particles hold significant weight. To mitigate this, a resampling step is performed periodically (e.g., when the Effective Sample Size  $ N_{eff} = 1 / \sum_{i=1}^{N} (w_t^{(i)})^2 $ falls below a threshold). The objective of the resampling step is to counteract weight degeneracy by replicating particles with high importance weights and discarding those with low weights. This process results in a new set of  $ N $ particles  $ \{\hat{x}_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N $, each assigned a uniform weight of  $ 1/N $. Common resampling algorithms include multinomial resampling and systematic resampling.

#### 3.3.2 Gradient based optimization methods

##### 3.3.2.1 Gradient descent

Gradient Descent stands as a fundamental and widely adopted iterative algorithm for parameter estimation in statistical and machine learning models. The core principle of Gradient Descent is to iteratively minimize a loss function  $ \mathcal{L}(\theta) $ that quantifies the discrepancy between the model's predictions and the observed data. Beginning with an initial parameter vector  $ \theta_0 $, the algorithm proceeds by repeatedly adjusting the parameters in the direction opposite to the gradient of the loss function. The parameter update at iteration  $ k $ is given by:

 $$ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\cdot\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k}), $$ 

where the learning rate  $ \eta > 0 $ is a crucial hyperparameter that controls the step size of each update, and  $ \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta) $ represents the gradient vector of the objective function with respect to the parameters.

##### 3.3.2.2 The adaptive moment estimation optimizer

To obtain maximum likelihood estimates for the volatility model, this study employs the Adaptive moment estimation (Adam) optimization algorithm. Adam minimizes the negative log-likelihood function by adaptively adjusting the learning rate for each parameter  $ \theta $. This adjustment is governed by two moving averages of the gradients: the first-order moment  $ m_{t} $ (the mean) and the uncentered second-order moment  $ v_{t} $ (the variance). Such a mechanism accelerates convergence and ensures stability during the optimization process within the high-dimensional parameter spaces typical of complex financial models. The update rules for the objective function  $ \mathcal{L}(\theta) $ are defined as follows:

 $$ \begin{array}{c}{m_{t}=\beta_{1}m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{1})g_{t}}\\ {v_{t}=\beta_{2}v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})g_{t}^{2}}\\ {\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{v_{t}}+\epsilon}m_{t}}\\ \end{array} $$ 

where  $  g_t = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)  $ is the gradient at time step  $  t  $,  $  \eta  $ is the initial learning rate,  $  \beta_1, \beta_2 \in [0,1)  $ are the exponential decay rates for the moment estimates, and  $  \epsilon  $ is a small scalar

(e.g.,  $ 10^{-8} $) to prevent division by zero. In this empirical setup, we set  $ \beta_{1}=0.9 $,  $ \beta_{2}=0.999 $, and  $ \epsilon=10^{-8} $, following the recommendation Kingma and Ba (2014). For both simulated and real datasets, we empirically determined the optimal learning rate to be  $ \eta=0.001 $.

##### 3.3.2.3 LASSO regularization and the Soft-Thresholding operator

In the GARCH-MIDAS framework, the MIDAS component typically incorporates numerous weight coefficients to capture information from low-frequency variables. To ensure model parsimony and enhance interpretability, we employ the Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regularization directly onto the parameter vector of the MIDAS component. This technique facilitates simultaneous variable selection and coefficient shrinkage via the  $ L_{1} $ norm, effectively mitigating overfitting. Formally, let  $ \theta $ represent the concatenated vector of GARCH, MIDAS, and neural network parameters. The LASSO-regularized estimation is defined as:

 $$ \hat{\theta}=\operatorname{a r g m i n}_{\theta}\{\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\lambda\parallel\theta_{\mathcal{S}}\parallel_{1}\},(12) $$ 

where $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ is the negative log-likelihood of volatility model, $\lambda \geq 0$ is the regularization parameter, and $\|\theta_\mathcal{S}\|_1 = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} |\theta_j|$ is the $L_1$-norm penalty applied to a subset $\mathcal{S}$ of parameters.

The core computational mechanism of LASSO is the soft-thresholding operator  $ S_{\lambda}(\cdot) $, which emerges directly from the optimality conditions of Eq (12). For a scalar input v and threshold  $ \tau = \lambda $, it is defined as:

 $$ S_{\lambda}(v)=sign(v)\cdot max(|v|-\lambda,0)=\begin{cases}v-\lambda&if v>\lambda,\\0&if|v|\leq\lambda,\\v+\lambda&if v<-\lambda.\end{cases} $$ 

In practice, we implement this operator via the efficient soft threshold function. This function constitutes the proximal operator for the  $ L_{1} $-norm and is used within iterative optimization algorithms (e.g., proximal gradient descent) to update parameters as follows:

 $$ \theta^{(k+1)}=S_{\lambda}\big(\theta_{(k)}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(k)})\big), $$ 

where  $ \eta $ is the learning rate. The operator’s thresholding behavior is crucial: it drives parameters with magnitude below  $ \lambda $ exactly to zero (achieving sparsity), while shrinking larger parameters toward zero (ensuring regularization).

#### 3.3.3 Forecast evaluation

To evaluate predictive performance, this study employs five predictive scores for both point and density forecasts. The first is the partial predictive score (PPS),

 $$ P P S=-\frac{1}{T_{t e s t}}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+T_{t e s t}}\log\ p(y_{t}|y_{1:t-1}), $$ 

where  $ T_{test} $ denotes the number of observations in the test sample. The second is quantile score, as defined in J. W. Taylor (2019):

 $$ Q S=\frac{1}{T_{t e s t}}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+T_{t e s t}}(\alpha-I_{y_{t}\leq q_{t,\alpha}})(y_{t}-q_{t,\alpha}) $$ 

where  $ q_{t,a} $ represents the  $ \alpha $-VaR forecast of  $ y_t $, conditional on  $ y_{1:t-1} $. Lower quantile score values indicate the better the Value-at-Risk forecast. The remaining three predictive scores compare the model-based volatility forecast  $ \widehat{v}_t^2 $ with the realized volatility in the test data (Hansen & Lunde, 2005):

 $$ M S E=\frac{1}{T_{t e s t}}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+T_{t e s t}}(\sqrt{R V}_{t}-\widehat{\vartheta_{t}})^{2}, $$ 

 $$ \begin{array}{r}{M A E=\frac{1}{T_{t e s t}}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+T_{t e s t}}|\sqrt{R V}_{t}-\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}|,}\end{array} $$ 

 $$ R^{2}LOG=\frac{1}{T_{test}}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+T_{test}}\log\left(RV_{t}/\widehat{\vartheta_{t}^{2}}\right)^{2} $$ 

Where  $ \widehat{v}_{t}^{2} = \nu \widehat{\sigma}_{t}^{2} / (\nu - 2) $ with  $ \widehat{\sigma}_{t}^{2} $ forecasted from the volatility model under Student's  $ T(v) $ innovations.

### 3.4 Proposed models

#### 3.4.1. Deep learning enhanced volatility modeling with covariates

##### 3.4.1.1 Motivation and contribution

The motivation for developing the RECH-X model lies in the inherent limitation of the baseline RECH model, which relies solely on the historical dynamics of the dependent variable to capture volatility patterns. While recurrent neural networks, particularly the RECH architecture are capable of modeling complex temporal dependencies, they do not inherently accommodate exogenous information that may contain valuable predictive signals for volatility. In the context of financial volatility forecasting, where a wide range of domestic macroeconomic indicators and global financial variables are known to influence market risk, excluding such covariates may result in suboptimal predictive performance and an incomplete understanding of the drivers of volatility.

The RECH-X model addresses this gap by extending the RECH framework to incorporate exogenous covariates that share the same frequency as the dependent variable (daily). This extension allows the model to jointly learn from both the historical sequence of the target variable and the contemporaneous information embedded in relevant predictors. The primary contribution of the proposed model is twofold. First, it provides a systematic evaluation of whether augmenting a recurrent volatility model with additional covariates specifically domestic macroeconomic versus global financial indicators enhances out-of-sample forecasting accuracy. Second, by maintaining the same temporal frequency for both the target and the covariates, the RECH-X model offers a straightforward yet effective mechanism for integrating exogenous information

into a deep learning-based volatility forecasting framework, facilitating direct comparison with the baseline RECH model to isolate the marginal predictive gains attributable to the inclusion of external predictors. In doing so, the model contributes to the growing literature on hybrid approaches to volatility forecasting by demonstrating the value of incorporating multi-source information within a recurrent architecture.

##### 3.4.1.2 RECH-X

This research focuses on testing the ability of the RECH model when incorporating exogenous information. To avoid potential confounding effects from a sophisticated LSTM architecture and to enhance model interpretability, we opt for a simple RNN instead of an LSTM. The research employs a GARCH(1,1) model for the volatility component with Student's T(v) innovations. The full model specification is as follows:

 $$ y_{t}=\sigma_{t}\varepsilon_{t},\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathrm{T}(\nu),t=1,2,...,T, $$ 

 $$ \sigma_{t}^{2}=\omega_{t}+\alpha y_{t}^{2}_{-1}+\beta\sigma_{t}^{2}_{-1},t=2,...,T, $$ 

 $$ \omega_{t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}h_{t},t=1,\ldots,T, $$ 

 $$ h_{t}=\Psi(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+w_{h}h_{t-1}+b),t=2,...,T, $$ 

Here,  $ \Psi(\cdot) $ is a non-linear activation function, which is chosen to be the ReLU  $ \Psi(x) = \max\{x, 0\} $. The GARCH parameters  $ \alpha $ and  $ \beta $ are imposed the usual constraint that  $ \alpha, \beta > 0 $ and  $ \alpha\frac{\nu}{\nu-2} + \beta < 1 $, which guarantees that the volatility  $ \sigma_t^2 $ is positive and finite for all  $ t $, see Nguyen et al. (2022).

First, set the initial  $ \sigma_1^2 $ following the same procedure as outlined above. The input vector  $ x_t = (\omega_{t-1}, y_{t-1}, \sigma_{t-1}^2) $ in Nguyen et al. (2022) contains the past information of the level of long-term volatility, return, and total volatility. However, it is easy to incorporate exogenous variables into its recurrent component whenever such inputs are available and useful in terms of modeling and predicting the distribution of  $ y_t $. Hence, with the availability of an exogenous variable  $ z_{t-1} $ at time  $ t $, the research proposes to use  $ x_t = (\omega_{t-1}, y_{t-1}, \sigma_{t-1}^2, z_{t-1})^\top $ and refer to the resulting model in (2a) - (2d) as RECH-X. For example, with the choice of  $ z_{t-1} $ as  $ RV_{t-1} $, the vector  $ v $ in (2d) has four coefficients and we denote by  $ v_\omega $,  $ v_y $,  $ v_\sigma $,  $ v_{RV} $ the coefficients with respect to  $ \omega_{t-1} $,  $ y_{t-1} $,  $ \sigma_{t-1}^2 $,  $ RV_{t-1} $, respectively. The magnitude of  $ v_{RV} $ characterizes the importance of realized measures for modeling the daily volatility  $ \sigma_t^2 $. The results on empirical data sets show that  $ v_{RV} $ is always significant. Furthermore, when multiple exogenous variables are used as,  $ z_t $ and its corresponding coefficients  $ v_z $ become vectors. The flexible structure of RECH-X allows it to be able to capture the non-linear and long-term effects that the exogenous variables have on the volatility.

When high-frequency data are available, Liu et al. (2023) extend the RealGARCH model by incorporating an RNN, similar to the approach used in RECH,

and name their model Real-RECH. The RECH-X model, however, differs from Real-RECH in a significant way. Specifically, RECH-X adopts a distinct strategy by directly integrating exogenous variables into the volatility equation, instead of introducing a separate measurement equation for realized volatility as in Real-RECH. This design is intended to accommodate the inclusion of diverse exogenous variables, which may not be feasibly incorporated into a measurement equation. Consequently, RECH-X offers greater flexibility, enabling market participants to model volatility using timely available related variables.

The Bayesian approach is adopted for inference and prediction in this thesis. The priors for the RECH-X parameters follow those of Nguyen et al. (2022). In particular, the priors of the parameters in the GARCH component are  $ \alpha \sim U(0,1) $ and  $ \beta \sim U(0,1) $; the priors of the coefficients  $ \beta_0 $ and  $ \beta_1 $ are  $ \beta_0 \sim U(0,0.5) $ and  $ \beta_1 \sim U(0,0.5) $; and this thesis uses a normal prior with a zero mean and variance 0.1 for the recurrence parameters of the RNN component,  $ \nu_\omega $,  $ \nu_y $,  $ \nu_\sigma $,  $ \nu_z $, b, as they are often small. The prior for the regressors coefficients  $ \nu_z $ in (2d) is  $ N(0,0.5) $. Lastly, the prior of the degrees of freedom is  $ v \sim \Gamma(\alpha,1) $. For the parameters of the GARCH and the RealGARCH model, the research employs the commonly used priors in the literature (Gerlach & Wang, 2016).

Due to the complex nature of the RECH-X model and its challenging posterior distribution, the research uses the likelihood-annealing SMC method (Duan & Fulop, 2015) to sample from the posterior. Besides, the likelihood-annealing SMC also provides an estimation of the log marginal likelihood for the model comparison.

Let  $ y = \{y_t, t = 1, \cdots T\} $ be the training data and  $ \theta $ the set of model parameters. The likelihood annealing SMC samples sequentially from the prior distribution  $ p(\theta) $ to the posterior distribution  $ p(\theta|y) \propto p(y|\theta)p(\theta) $ through a sequence of annealed distributions,

 $$ \pi_{k}(\theta)\propto p(\theta)p(y|\theta)^{a k},k=1,...,K $$ 

with $0 = a_1 < a_2 < \cdots < a_K = 1$. The samples drawn from the last annealed distribution $\pi_K(\theta)$ are from the posterior $p(\theta|y)$.

To perform expanding-window out-of-sample prediction, which requires updating the posterior distribution upon the arrival of each new observation, we utilize a data-annealing SMC sampler. This approach efficiently samples from the sequence of posterior distributions induced by the expanding data window.

 $$ \pi_{t}(\theta)\propto p(\theta)p(y_{1},...,y_{T+t}|\theta),t=1,2,... $$

#### b. The impact of financial and economic indicators on volatility forecasting

Evidence from prior studies indicates that stock market volatility is shaped by key financial-economic indicators, namely economic growth, exchange rates, gold and oil prices, interest rates, inflation, and money supply. Moreover, global stock market fluctuations may also affect other countries' markets through spillover effects. This

study specifically investigates the impact of exchange rates, gold prices, oil prices, and U.S. stock market uncertainty on the volatility of five markets: Vietnam (VN100), Japan (N225), France (CAC40), Australia (ASX), and Brazil (BVSP). Other macroeconomic variables such as GDP may also be considered. These four factors are used as the inputs  $ z_{t} $ in the RECH-X model. Importantly, exogenous variables fed into the RNN must be standardized. Specifically, the in-sample data for each input time series are standardized to have zero mean and unit variance, with the same mean and standard deviation applied to normalize the out-of-sample data.

Exchange rates: Empirical evidence on the impact of exchange rates on stock market volatility remains inconclusive. Abugri (2008) and Tursoy et al. (2008) find that exchange rates have no significant effect on stock market volatility. In contrast, Adam & Tweneboah (2008) report a negative effect, whereas Maysami et al. (2004) support the view of a positive relationship between exchange rates and stock returns.

Gold: Gold and stocks represent two alternative investment options frequently considered by investors. Gold is widely viewed as a safe-haven asset that provides financial stability during economic downturns, whereas stocks offer the potential for higher long-term returns but entail greater risk and uncertainty. Smith (2001) demonstrates bidirectional causality between gold and stock prices using the Granger causality test. Garefalakis et al. (2011) contend that volatility in gold prices adversely affects investment returns in the Hong Kong stock market. Akbar et al. (2019) apply a vector autoregressive model to demonstrate an inverse relationship between gold prices and stock prices, while Singhal et al. (2019) show that the world gold prices exert a positive influence on Mexico's stock market. Collectively, these studies offer valuable insights into the complex relationship between gold and stocks across different contexts.

Crude oil: Empirical findings on the linkage between oil and stock prices have varied, revealing either positive or negative associations depending on the markets under consideration. For instance, Singhal et al. (2019) find that oil prices exert a negative impact on stock prices of Mexico, whereas M. I. Khan et al. (2021) and Mokni & Youssef. (2019) show a positive relationship between crude oil price and stock market performance.

Uncertainty in the US stock market: The VIX is a widely used indicator of volatility expectations derived from S&P 500 index options, commonly employed to capture uncertainty in the US stock market. Given the interconnected nature of global financial markets, fluctuations in US stock market volatility, as captured by the VIX, are generally expected to affect the volatility of other countries' equity markets. Moreover, the high persistence typically observed in financial returns volatility reinforces the idea

that the VIX may exert a positive influence on the long-term volatility of other national stock markets.

#### c. Results analysis

This section introduces the RECH-X model, a deep learning-enhanced volatility framework that integrates high-frequency data along with financial and economic information as exogenous variables. First, regarding model performance, the proposed RECH-X model demonstrates superiority over standard benchmarks such as GARCH and GARCH-X. The log marginal likelihood estimates provide strong evidence in favor the RECH-X model, particularly when exogenous variables are incorporated, confirming the advantage of

using recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to capture complex nonlinear relationships between macroeconomic factors and market volatility.

Second, the degrees of freedom in the Student’s T-distribution of the RECH-X model are larger than those of the GARCH and GARCH-X models. This implies that the RECH-X model captures a thinner-tailed return distribution, suggesting it is less prone to over-predicting extreme volatility than traditional models. This finding indicates that RECH-X not only forecasts volatility more accurately but also assesses tail risk in a more balanced and reliable manner a critical factor in asset pricing and portfolio management.

Third, concerning the impact of variables, U.S. market volatility (VIX) is the strongest and most consistent factor, positively affecting volatility across all studied markets, which reinforces the global financial spillover effect. Gold and oil prices exhibit heterogeneous impacts dependent on specific market characteristics: while most markets react negatively to rising oil prices, Vietnam shows a positive correlation. #### 3.4.2. Variable selection in mixed-frequency deep learning volatility models

##### 3.4.2.1 Motivation and contribution

Volatility serves as the cornerstone for a wide range of financial applications, including but not limited to derivative valuation, risk assessment, and strategic asset allocation. Time-varying volatility exhibits complex characteristics that cannot be fully captured by simple statistical models, such as clustering (Mandelbrot,1967), heavy-tailed distributions (Carnero et al., 2004), nonlinearity and long-memory auto-dependence (Nguyen et al., 2023). Therefore, these attributes require the deployment of advanced volatility models to facilitate informed decisions in uncertain environments.

The seminal work of Engle (1982) on ARCH model and its generalization by Bollerslev (1986) to the GARCH framework provided a pivotal solution to these issues.

Engle et al. (2013) introduced GARCH-MIDAS volatility model that integrates data sampled at different frequencies to account for the long-run component of volatility. Because of these capabilities, GARCH-MIDAS has gained increasing attention and has become an important tool in the recent volatility forecasting literature (e.g., Wang et al. (2020); Song et al. (2023)). The model's strengths are significant, yet it presents serious estimation problems owing to its high parameter dimensionality, which follows directly from the lag structure of the independent variables. To reduce this complexity and increase model parsimony, techniques for selecting variables including the Lasso method adopted by Fang et al. (2020) offer an effective means of efficiently isolating relevant predictors.

This research proposes an extension of the GARCH-MIDAS model by integrating a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). The approach builds upon the rationale of the RECH model introduced by Nguyen et al. (2022) where the proposed model leverages the GARCH-MIDAS framework to separate the volatility components and enhances it with the LSTM predictive power. Model estimation with LASSO regularization Tibshirani (1996) aims to improve parsimony, forecast accuracy, and interpretability. The main contributions of this research are summarized as follows:

First, a novel class of hybrid models is introduced: the RNN-GARCH-MIDASLASSO with its variants using LSTM and GRU. These models extend the standard GARCH-MIDAS framework by simultaneously integrating deep learning architectures to capture complex temporal patterns and employing LASSO regularization to achieve automated, parsimonious feature selection, thus improving overall modeling capability.

Second, an efficient optimization algorithm is employed to fit the model. The proposed algorithm builds on established techniques, including proximal gradient methods and adaptive learning, and is tailored to enable simultaneous model estimation and feature selection within the framework. Extensive simulation studies are then performed, validating the proposed models and examine their estimation properties. These studies provide critical evidence on parameter identifiability, algorithmic performance, and the practical feasibility of the proposed approach.

Third, the proposed LSTM-GARCH-MIDAS-LASSO model demonstrates its forecasting superiority through a comprehensive empirical validation. Benchmarking against key alternatives (GARCH, GARCH-MIDAS, RECH) across five major global equity indices (DJI, DAX, BVSP, AORD, N225) confirms its robust performance and establishes the generalizability of the results under varying market conditions.

##### 3.4.2.2 Deep learning enhanced GARCH-MIDAS

Inspired by the architectural strengths of the RECH model, this study proposes a novel hybrid GARCH-MIDAS extension. The proposed model, which integrates the GARCH-MIDAS framework and deep learning architectures, is designated as RNN-GARCH-MIDAS. The nomenclature adapts according to the neural network architecture employed, such as LSTM or GRU to capture the nonlinear dynamics within the short-term volatility component. The structural specification of the model is formally defined by the following equations:

 $$ y_{i,t}=\sqrt{\tau_{t}g_{i,t}}\epsilon_{i,t},\qquad t=1,2,\ldots,T;i=1,\ldots,N_{t};\epsilon_{i,t}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1,t}\sim N(0,1) $$ 

 $$ g_{i,t}=\omega_{i,t}+\alpha\frac{y_{i-1,t}^{\varepsilon}}{\tau_{t}}+\beta g_{i-1,t},\qquad t=1,2,\ldots,T;i=2,\ldots,N_{t}, $$ 

 $$ \log(\tau_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{k}\left(\gamma_{j,1},\gamma_{j,2}\right)X_{j,t-k},\qquad t=2,\ldots,T, $$ 

 $$ \omega_{i,t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}h_{i,t},\ t=1,\ldots,T, $$ 

 $$ h_{i,t}=\mathrm{RNN}(x_{i,t},h_{i-1,t}),\quad t=2,\ldots,T,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad h_{1}\equiv0, $$ 

where the standard GARCH constraints  $ \alpha > 0, \beta > 0 $ and  $ \alpha + \beta < 1 $, ensuring positive and finite conditional variance  $ \sigma_t^2 $ remains for all  $ t $. Here, activation function is the ReLU. The input vector  $ x_{i,t} = (\omega_{i-1,t}, y_{i-1,t}, g_{i-1,t}, \tau_{t-1}) $, following the specification of, incorporates lagged information regarding long-term volatility, short-term volatility, and historical returns. The weights  $ \phi_k(\gamma_{j,1}, \gamma_{j,2}) $ are determined by the formula and  $ \gamma_{j,1}, \gamma_{j,2} $ parameters.

Furthermore, this research extends experimental framework by integrating the GARCH-MIDAS model with alternative neural network architectures, including LSTM and GRU. We then propose a unified estimation algorithm to evaluate these hybrid models, with the ultimate aim of comparing their performance and selecting the most suitable one.

##### 3.4.2.3 Model estimation

The proposed hybrid model features a complex structure with a large number of parameters, posing a significant challenge for estimation. To address this issue, we effectively integrate advanced optimization techniques and develop an efficient optimization algorithm for model fitting, based on the proximal gradient method and adaptive learning.

The parameters of the hybrid model are estimated by maximizing the following penalized log-likelihood function. This function combines the conventional GARCH log-likelihood with an L1-norm (LASSO) regularization term to induce variable selection as follows:

 $$ \mathrm{PLLF}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\left[\log(2\pi)+\log(\sigma_{i,t}^{2})+\frac{y_{i,t}^{2}}{\sigma_{i,t}^{2}}\right]-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{J}\widehat{w_{j}}\mid\theta_{j}\mid $$ 

where $\lambda > 0$ is the regularization parameter controlling the penalty strength. The term $\widehat{w}_j$ represents the adaptive weight for $\theta_j$. To obtain these adaptive weights, we first estimate a GARCH-MIDAS model using all $J$ exogenous variables, obtaining preliminary estimates $\widehat{\theta}_j$ from maximizing equation under the constraints of $\alpha > 0$, $\beta > 0$ and $\alpha + \beta < 1$. The adaptive weights are then computed as $\widehat{w}_j = 1/|\widehat{\theta}_j|^n$, where $\eta$ is a positive constant. This is the standard method in the literature for choosing the adaptive weights.

This study performs a search for hyperparameter  $ \lambda $ based on the BIC criterion to find the optimal model. For each  $ \lambda $ value within a pre-specified range, the model is estimated and evaluated, and then the model with the lowest BIC is selected.

In short, this approach introduces a dual-mechanism RNN-GARCH-MIDAS-LASSO framework: leveraging LASSO regularization for automated feature selection and complexity control, while embedding a deep learning architecture to capture high-dimensional nonlinearities. This synergy is expected to yield a model that is both computationally parsimonious and transitionally robust, ultimately enhancing both interpret ability and out-of-sample forecasting precision.

##### 3.4.2.4 Empirical analysis

This section consists of two parts. First, in the simulation study, we evaluate the proposed models and their estimation procedures, providing evidence on parameter recovery, algorithmic performance, and the practical feasibility of the proposed approach. Each model is treated as the true data-generating process to simulate 30 datasets, which are then used to assess the ability of the estimation algorithm to recover the true parameters. Parameter estimation accuracy and variable selection performance are evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE) and the correctly fitted percentage. We further compare the three proposed hybrid architectures to identify the most effective specification. The results indicate that the LSTM–GARCH–MIDAS–LASSO model achieves the best overall performance on the simulated data.

Second, in the real-data application, we examine the performance of the proposed method using several real datasets. The empirical analysis is based on data from the Oxford–Man Institute, covering stock indices and exogenous variables from five major markets: the United States (Dow Jones Industrial Average, DJI), Brazil (São Paulo Bovespa Index, BVSP), Germany (Deutscher Aktienindex, DAX), Australia (All Ordinaries Index, AORD), and Japan (Nikkei 225 Stock Average, NK225). We assess the forecasting performance of the LSTM–GARCH–MIDAS–LASSO model and benchmark it against standard models, including GARCH, RECH, and GARCH–MIDAS.

#### b. Real-data applications

This research relies on secondary data sourced from Macrobond, a global provider of comprehensive economic and financial analytical databases. The dataset comprises the daily closing prices of five major national stock indices, namely the United States, Germany, Japan, Australia, and Brazil, alongside two key macroeconomic indicators (industrial production and unemployment rate), and the market uncertainty gauge (e.g., VIX as the U.S. volatility index). The selection of covariates in this study is grounded in well-established theoretical and empirical foundations from the literature.

Hamilton and Lin (1996) demonstrate that stock market volatility intensifies during economic recessions, where macroeconomic indicators such as the unemployment rate and industrial production serve as key barometers of economic conditions. Engle and Rangel (2008), by employing the Spline-GARCH model, establish a significant correlation between the low-frequency volatility component and intrinsic economic fundamentals, including inflation, GDP growth, and short-term interest rates.

Subsequent empirical research further corroborates this link. Girardin and Joyeux (2015), for instance, confirm the significant explanatory power of inflation and industrial production for market volatility in China using a GARCH-MIDAS framework. Similarly, Baroian (2014) identifies a substantial impact of the unemployment rate on stock market risk in Eastern European economies.

Finally, this study incorporates the volatility index to account for volatility spillovers and global financial sentiment. In the context of deep financial integration, the volatility index not only serves as a primary gauge of market risk but also acts as a source of transmitted shocks that exert a persistent influence on the long-term volatility components of emerging markets. ### 3.5. Forecasting volatility in the Vietnamese Stock Market: an application of the proposed models

This subsection undertakes an empirical application of the proposed models to forecast volatility in the Vietnamese stock market, thereby providing a substantive context for evaluating their predictive performance. As an emerging frontier market, Vietnam exhibits structural characteristics, which including progressive financial integration, evolving institutional frameworks, and pronounced sensitivity to both domestic policy shifts and external financial spillovers that render volatility forecasting a methodologically intricate and practically salient endeavor.

The Vietnamese stock market, despite its rapid development, remains characterized by shallower liquidity, higher information asymmetry, and greater susceptibility to sentiment-driven fluctuations relative to developed counterparts, necessitating forecasting approaches capable of capturing the complex interplay between persistent macroeconomic fundamentals and transient market dynamics. Applying the suite of proposed models comprising the baseline RECH architecture, the RECH-X extension incorporating same frequency exogenous covariates, and additional hybrid specifications this empirical investigation seeks to rigorously assess their relative out-of-sample forecasting accuracy within this distinct market setting.

Beyond serving as a robustness test of the models' generalizability, the analysis directly addresses the central research question concerning the differential predictive contributions of domestic macroeconomic indicators versus global financial variables. The findings are anticipated to yield both methodological insights into the efficacy of hybrid volatility frameworks in emerging market contexts and practical implications for risk management practices and policy formulation in Vietnam.

#### 3.5.1 Forecasting Stock market volatility: a review and empirical approach

Numerous studies have discussed the effectiveness of GARCH models in explaining stock market volatility. Choudhry (1997) examined volatility in the stock markets of Canada, Denmark, Sweden, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, and the United States, as well as the impact of short-term changes among these markets' stock indices on volatility during the period from January 1926 to December 1944. The empirical investigation employed GARCH(1,1) and GARCH(1,1)-X models, revealing

a significant impact of short-term changes among the stock indices on volatility, with the GARCH(1,1)-X model providing superior analytical results. Karmakar (2005) investigated volatility in the Indian stock market, demonstrating that the GARCH(1,1) model provided reasonably accurate market volatility forecasts, which were then applied to 50 individual stocks.

Similarly, Chand et al. (2012) identified the GARCH(1,1) model as the most effective forecasting model. Tran and Do (2013) measured the volatility of the VN-Index using GARCH models with daily closing price data from 2000 to 2011. The authors employed GARCH(p,q) models under various error distribution assumptions, including normal, Student's t, generalized error, and skewed Student's t distributions. The findings indicated that the GARCH(1,1) model with normally distributed errors produced the most accurate forecasts. Ugurlu et al. (2014) studied volatility in European emerging stock markets, specifically Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland, and Turkey using daily closing price data from Reuters covering the period 2001–2012. The results revealed persistent effects of shocks on return volatility and asymmetric responses to news.

Pham (2017) also reported stock price volatility forecasts using GARCH models based on data from 2006 to 2016, concluding that past returns significantly determine current returns. Hasan and Zaman (2017) examined the responsiveness of Bangladesh's stock market return volatility to macroeconomic variables, utilizing monthly index data from the Dhaka Stock Exchange (DSE) and four macroeconomic variables (call money rate, crude oil price, exchange rate, and the SENSEX from the Bombay Stock Exchange) over the period January 2001 to December 2015. The GARCH-S model results indicated that DSE return volatility was significantly influenced by macroeconomic variables, particularly the exchange rate and SENSEX.

Robiyanto et al. (2019) investigated the impact of macroeconomic variables namely the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the USD/IDR exchange rate, and global crude oil prices on the Jakarta Composite Index (IHSG) during 2005–2016 using the GARCH(1,1) model. Their findings showed that the Dow Jones Industrial Average and global crude oil prices positively affected the IHSG, while the USD/IDR exchange rate had a negative impact.

Therefore, this study will employ proposed hybrid models integrated with standard models to forecast the volatility of the VN-Index and HNX-Index based on four macroeconomic indicators: crude oil prices, the USD/VND exchange rate, Bitcoin prices, and global gold prices (USD).

#### 3.5.2 Overview of the Vietnamese Stock Market

The Vietnamese stock market, established in July 2000 with its first trading session at the Ho Chi Minh City Stock Exchange, has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past quarter century. What began with just two listed companies, REE and

SAM, and a market capitalization of less than 1 percent of GDP has grown into a vital component of the national financial system. Today, the market comprises two main stock exchanges: the Ho Chi Minh City Stock Exchange (HOSE), which serves as the largest exchange listing primarily large cap blue chip stocks, and the Hanoi Stock Exchange (HNX), which hosts small and medium sized enterprises along with government bonds and derivatives. Additionally, the Unlisted Public Company Market (UPCoM), operated by HNX, provides a trading platform for unlisted public companies.

As of July 2025, the total market capitalization of the Vietnamese stock market reached approximately 8.2 million billion VND, equivalent to nearly 71 percent of GDP, the highest level in history. The number of listed and registered companies has exceeded 1,600, covering virtually all key economic sectors, while the number of investor accounts has surpassed 10 million, reflecting the market's widespread reach across society. Trading liquidity has improved substantially, with billion dollar trading sessions becoming commonplace, positioning Vietnam among the top markets in ASEAN in terms of market depth.

A notable feature of the market is the significant concentration of capitalization within major private business ecosystems. As of early 2026, approximately 31 percent of the total market capitalization is held by six major corporate groups: Vingroup, Hòa Phát, Masan Group, FPT, GELEX, and Sovico. The Vingroup ecosystem alone accounts for roughly 10 percent of the entire market.

The market is currently classified as a "frontier market" by MSCI and FTSE Russell, though the Vietnamese government has been actively pursuing an upgrade to emerging market status, implementing decisive solutions to meet international standards and attract greater foreign investment.

#### 3.5.3 Data descriptions

The study employs two primary stock market indices in Vietnam: the VN-Index and the HNX-Index. The VN-Index reflects price movements of stocks listed on the Ho Chi Minh City Stock Exchange (HOSE), while the HNX-Index captures price fluctuations of stocks listed on the Hanoi Stock Exchange. In addition, data on four macroeconomic variables, including crude oil prices, the USD/VND exchange rate, Bitcoin prices, and global gold prices (USD), are collected to serve as exogenous variables in the GARCH-X model. The dataset comprises 2,262 observations spanning from January 2016 to January 2025. The sample is divided into two subsets: a training set consisting of the first 1,500 observations, and a testing set containing the remaining observations. All data were obtained from https://vn.investing.com, a leading global platform for online financial data and news. Empirical analyses were conducted using Excel and Matlab 2024.

For time series data, the series used for forecasting must be stationary. Therefore, to forecast stock market volatility, this study tests for stationarity in the data series and transforms non-stationary series into stationary ones. The original VN-Index and HNX-Index price series are transformed into return series, denoted as  $ r_t $, and calculated using the following formula:  $ r_t = 100 \log(P_t / P_{t-1}) $, where  $ P_t $ is the closing price of the series at time  $ t $. The macroeconomic indicator series are transformed into growth series using the formula  $ 100 \times (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1} $. Using unit root tests, the results indicate that all transformed series are stationary at the 1% significance level. The mean of the return series  $ r_t $ is denoted as  $ \mu_t = E(r_t \mid F_{t-1}) $, which is assumed to be zero, where  $ F_{t-1} $ is the information set available up to time  $ t - 1 $. Accordingly, the  $ r_t $ series is adjusted to have zero expectation by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation. The conditional variance of the return series  $ r_t $ is denoted as  $ \sigma_t^2 = \text{Var}(r_t \mid F_{t-1}) $.
